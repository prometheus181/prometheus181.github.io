--- name: ux-motion description: Use when building or reviewing UI animation, transitions, hover and press states, menus, modals, toasts, drag interactions, or any interface where something moves. Applies 20 animation and UX principles (squash and stretch through frequency of use) with concrete timings, easings and sizes, and flags where a design breaks them. --- # UX motion: 20 principles Use this whenever you build or review an interface that moves. Every principle below has a number you can apply directly. Pick the ones that fit the moment. Most screens need four or five, not all twenty. When you build, apply the rules quietly and mention the important choices in one line each. When you review, list each problem with the principle it breaks, where it happens, and the fix. ## 1. Squash and stretch Things squish on impact, which gives them weight. On a bounce or a press, squash one axis and stretch the other so the area stays about the same (scaleX 1.1 with scaleY 0.9). Keep it between 80 and 150ms. Use it on physical, playful elements like toggles, like buttons and draggable cards. Never squash text, numbers or data. ## 2. Anticipation A small move in the opposite direction right before the main action, like a crouch before a jump. A send button can swing back before it flies off; a card can dip before it gets flung away. The wind-up runs 60 to 200ms and travels roughly 5 to 25 percent of the main move. Save it for launches and big moves. A plain button doesn't need one. ## 3. Stagger Bring lists in one item after another instead of all at once. Use 30 to 60ms between items and keep the whole cascade under about 300ms. Past the first 6 to 8 items, show the rest together so long lists don't make people wait. ## 4. Follow through Parts keep moving after the main body stops, then settle. Let children lag the parent by 20 to 60ms, or use a spring with a small overshoot (damping ratio around 0.7 to 0.85). Good on sheets, cards with avatars, and anything with loose parts. ## 5. Slow in, slow out Nothing in real life moves at a constant speed. Use ease-out for things entering (`cubic-bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1)`), ease-in for things leaving (`cubic-bezier(0.7, 0, 0.84, 0)`), and ease-in-out for things moving from one spot on screen to another (`cubic-bezier(0.65, 0, 0.35, 1)`). Linear is only for spinners, progress bars and marquees. ## 6. Moving in arcs Things that travel diagonally should curve. Animate x and y with different easings (ease-out on one axis, ease-in on the other) and the path bends on its own. Straight diagonal lines look mechanical. Short moves under about 40px can stay straight. ## 7. Secondary action A small motion that backs up the main one: the heart fills while the count ticks up, the checkmark draws while the button turns green. It runs at the same time or right after, and it's always smaller and quieter than the main action. If it competes for attention, cut it. ## 8. Snappy timing Duration sets the feel. Small feedback like hover and press: 100 to 150ms. Most UI transitions: 150 to 300ms. Large panels and full-screen moves: up to about 400ms. Anything over 500ms feels slow unless it's a one-time moment like onboarding. ## 9. Exaggerated feedback Push it past real so the moment lands. A like can pop to 1.2x before settling, and a wrong password can shake 3 times over about 300ms. Keep it short and save it for moments that matter. If everything is exaggerated, nothing stands out. ## 10. Solid drawing in 3D Give flat things depth. Use layered shadows, a light highlight on top edges, and small perspective tilts (rotateX or rotateY up to about 10 to 15 degrees with `perspective` around 800 to 1200px). Cards that tilt toward the cursor and buttons that press down into the page both use this. ## 11. Origin awareness Things should grow from what you clicked. Set `transform-origin` to the trigger: a dropdown scales from its button, a popover from its anchor, a modal from the card that opened it. Radix exposes `--radix-popover-content-transform-origin` for this. Scaling from the center of the screen breaks the link between cause and effect. ## 12. Fitts's law Bigger, closer targets are faster to hit. Make touch targets at least 44 by 44pt (Apple) or 48 by 48dp (Material), even when the visible icon is smaller; pad the hit area instead. Put frequent actions near where the pointer or thumb already is. On desktop, screen edges and corners are the easiest targets of all. ## 13. Rubber banding When someone drags past the edge, stretch instead of stopping dead. Move the content by a shrinking fraction of the drag, for example `offset = (1 - 1 / (drag * 0.55 / size + 1)) * size`, then spring back on release. A hard wall feels broken; resistance tells people they hit the end. ## 14. Hick's law More choices mean slower decisions. Cut options, group the rest, and hide advanced settings behind a "More" or a second step. When one option is the right pick for most people, mark it as recommended. ## 15. Von Restorff The one that stands out gets remembered. Give the main action or the recommended plan a different color, size or treatment from everything around it. Only one thing per view gets this. Two highlighted items cancel each other out. ## 16. Doherty threshold Respond within 400ms or it feels slow, and show some feedback within 100ms. If the real work takes longer, update the UI right away (optimistic updates), show a skeleton, or show progress. Never leave a click with no visible response. ## 17. Interruptible motion Let people change their mind mid-animation. Animate from the current value, not from the start, and never block input while something moves. CSS transitions and springs (Motion, React Spring) retarget smoothly. CSS keyframe animations restart from the beginning, so avoid them for anything the user can reverse, like menus, drawers and toggles. ## 18. Fast closing Close faster than you open. When someone opens something, the motion shows where it came from. When they close it, they're already done with it, so get out of the way. Make exits about half to a third of the entry: open in 250ms with ease-out, close in 100 to 150ms with ease-in. ## 19. Peak-end rule People judge an experience by its best or worst moment and by how it ends. Give the end of a flow a real moment, like a checkmark that draws in, a short success animation, or a clear summary. Also find the most frustrating step and fix it first, because people remember that one too. ## 20. Frequency of use The more something is used, the less it should animate. A command palette opened 50 times a day should appear almost instantly with no flourish. Onboarding, first-time success and rare celebrations can take more time and more motion. Always respect `prefers-reduced-motion`: swap movement for a quick fade or no animation. ## Quick checks before you ship - Does every animation have a reason (feedback, orientation or emphasis)? - Are durations inside the ranges above, and does closing run faster than opening? - Does every popover, menu and modal grow from its trigger? - Can a user interrupt or reverse anything that moves? - Does the UI respond within 100ms to every click and tap? - Does it still work with `prefers-reduced-motion` turned on?

--- name: ui-principles description: Use when building or reviewing UI layouts, loading states, forms, drag and drop, menus, modals, toasts, progress, carousels, scrolling or any screen people tap and wait on. Applies 19 UX principles (visual stability through toast stacking) with concrete sizes, timings and thresholds, and flags where a design breaks them. --- # UI principles: 19 more Use this whenever you build or review an interface people click, drag, type into or wait on. Every principle below has a number or a rule you can apply directly. Pick the ones that fit the screen. Most screens need four or five, not all nineteen. When you build, apply the rules quietly and mention the important choices in one line each. When you review, list each problem with the principle it breaks, where it happens, and the fix. ## 1. Visual stability Don't let things jump while people aim. Save the space for anything that loads late (images, banners, ads, embeds) with a fixed width and height, `aspect-ratio` or `min-height`, and drop the content into that slot when it arrives. Never insert content above something the user is about to tap. Animate with `transform` and `opacity`, since changing `top`, `height` or `margin` pushes the layout around. Google counts a Cumulative Layout Shift of 0.1 or less as good and over 0.25 as poor. ## 2. Shared element transition The thing you tap should grow into the next screen. Move the same element's bounds and corner radius from the card to its spot on the new page, so only one copy of the image exists the whole way. Re-crop the image every frame with `object-fit: cover` so it never stretches. A full-screen zoom like this can run 400 to 500ms with a strong ease-out such as `cubic-bezier(0.32, 0.72, 0, 1)`. Closing reverses it back into the same card. Use the View Transitions API (`view-transition-name`), Motion's `layoutId`, or the iOS 18 zoom transition. With `prefers-reduced-motion`, crossfade instead. ## 3. Chunking Break long numbers into groups. Card numbers go 4 4 4 4 (Amex is 4 6 5), phone numbers follow the local format, and codes go in groups of 3 or 4. Insert the spaces automatically as people type so nothing typed earlier moves, keep the caret where it should be, accept pasted numbers with or without spaces, and strip the spaces before you validate. Use `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` so the digits line up. ## 4. Direct manipulation Whatever you drag stays under your finger. While the pointer is down, move the element by exactly the pointer's change in position every frame, 1 to 1, with no easing, spring or lerp in between. Keep the point you grabbed under the pointer instead of snapping the element's center to it. Use pointer events with `setPointerCapture` and move it with `transform`. Springs and momentum only start after release, from the release velocity. Give every drag a single-tap or keyboard alternative (WCAG 2.2, 2.5.7). ## 5. User control and freedom Don't ask "are you sure?" for things that can be undone. Do the action right away and show an Undo in a snackbar, with one action and a 4 to 10 second timeout (Material). Keep the real delete pending until the snackbar is gone, so Undo actually works. Save confirm dialogs for things that can't be undone, like deleting an account or sending money, and name the action on the button ("Delete 3 files") instead of "OK". People click through confirmations they see all the time. ## 6. Odometer effect When a number changes while people are watching, roll the digits so they can see which way it went. Give each digit its own column with a fixed width and `overflow: hidden`, use tabular figures so nothing jitters, and roll only the digits that changed. Roll up when the value rises and down when it falls. Around 300ms per digit with a small spring overshoot, starting from the right-most digit about 30ms apart, reads well. NumberFlow and Motion's AnimateNumber do this. Skip it for tables that refresh constantly, and just swap the number under `prefers-reduced-motion`. ## 7. Menu aim When the cursor cuts diagonally from a menu row to its submenu, it crosses other rows. Don't swap the submenu while the cursor is moving inside the triangle between its tip and the submenu's near top and bottom corners. If the cursor stops or leaves the triangle, switch after a short delay (Ben Kamens' menu-aim uses 300ms). Floating UI's `safePolygon` handles this for hover menus. On touch there is no hover, so submenus open on tap. ## 8. Skeleton screens When a whole page is loading, show its shape instead of a spinner. Draw the skeleton from the same sizes as the real layout so nothing moves when content arrives, then fade content into the exact slots over about 160ms with ease-out. Run one shimmer band across all the shapes at once, linear, left to right. Following Nielsen Norman: under about 1 second show nothing, a skeleton for a full page and a spinner for one small module up to about 10 seconds, and a progress bar beyond that. Under `prefers-reduced-motion`, drop the shimmer. ## 9. Elevation Things you pick up should lift off the page. On grab, scale to about 1.03 to 1.06 (past 1.08 it reads as a zoom), tilt 2 to 4 degrees and grow the shadow to something deep like `0 24px 40px rgba(0,0,0,.6), 0 8px 14px rgba(0,0,0,.4)`, on a spring that lands in about 150ms. Show a gap or placeholder where it will drop. On release, reverse it and ease into the slot over about 160ms. On dark interfaces a shadow barely shows, so also lighten the lifted surface, the way Material's dark theme does. ## 10. Scrim Dim everything behind a modal so there's only one place to look. Material uses black at 32% opacity. Dark interfaces need more, around 60 to 75%, because a dark page barely changes at 32%. Fade the scrim in with the dialog (about 200ms) and out faster. Clicking the scrim can close a dialog that doesn't hold unsaved work. Lock page scroll behind it. Don't put a scrim behind menus, popovers or toasts, since they don't block the page. ## 11. Shape morphing The button itself becomes the spinner, then the check. On press, fade the label out, shrink the width to the button's height and the radius to half the height so it becomes a circle (about 200ms ease-in-out), with the spinner inside. When it's done, spring it back to full width with a small overshoot, draw the check in with `stroke-dashoffset` over about 180ms ease-out, and bring the new label in. Keep the center fixed the whole time. While it's busy, block double submits and set `aria-busy`. If the work finishes in under about 1 second, skip the spinner and go straight to the check. ## 12. Illusion of completeness If a row ends exactly at the screen edge, people think that's all there is. Size the items so the last one is cut off, with about half of it showing (3.5 cards across instead of 3). Don't add a fade over it, the cut edge is the cue. The same goes for vertical pages: if a section ends right at the fold, people stop scrolling. Snap rows to item starts with `scroll-snap-type: x mandatory` and `scroll-snap-align: start`. ## 13. Inline validation Tick each rule off as people type instead of waiting for submit. Show the rules up front (8+ characters, a number, a symbol) and mark each one the moment it's met, with a small spring pop (0.6 to 1.15 to 1 over about 260ms). Never show red while someone is still typing in a field for the first time. Check for errors when they leave the field, then check live so the error clears as soon as it's fixed. Show messages in space you already saved so nothing shifts, never clear what they typed, and tie the message to the field with `aria-describedby`. ## 14. Inertial scrolling Flick something and it should keep gliding, then slow to a stop. On release, keep the release velocity (measured over the last 50 to 100ms of pointer movement) and decay it exponentially, fastest at the moment of release, with no ease-in. Apple's scroll views lose velocity at 0.998 per ms (normal) or 0.99 per ms (fast). To find where it will land, Apple's formula is `velocity / 1000 * rate / (1 - rate)`, with velocity in points per second. Motion's inertia defaults are power 0.8 and timeConstant 700. Native scrolling already does this, so only build it for custom surfaces like maps, canvases and carousels, and add rubber banding at the edges. ## 15. Change blindness People miss changes that happen away from where they're looking, like a cart count in the corner. Carry the eye there: fly a small copy of the item from the button to the cart (about 400ms, speeding up as it goes, shrinking to about 0.4), then give the target one pop (1 to 1.16 and back over about 200ms). One pop is enough. If you can't move the eye, put the feedback right where they tapped. Announce the change with an `aria-live` region for screen readers. ## 16. Progressive disclosure Show what most people need and tuck the rest behind "Advanced". Pick the few settings most people change (3 basics up front works well) and put the rest behind one row that says what's in it and how many, like "Advanced · 11 more". Defaults should be right for most people. Keep it to two levels, since Nielsen Norman found designs that go deeper than two usually get people lost. Never hide something people use every time. ## 17. Common fate Things that move together look like they belong together. When someone drags a selection of three items, move all three on the same path at the same speed on the same frame, keeping their layout, with no stagger and no lag between them. Anything that isn't part of the group should stay still. Stagger is for lists coming in, never for things the user is moving as one. ## 18. Visibility of system status If something takes a while, show how far along it is. Nielsen's limits: 0.1 seconds feels instant, 1 second keeps people's flow, and 10 seconds is about as long as they'll stay focused. A spinner is fine for a few seconds. Past about 10 seconds, show a progress bar with the percent, the amount done and an estimate ("about 5s left"), and let people cancel. Round the estimate and update the text in steps, not every frame. Progress should never move backwards, and progress that speeds up near the end feels shorter (Harrison et al., 2007). ## 19. Toast stacking When toasts pile up, stack them behind the newest so they don't cover the page. Follow Sonner's defaults: at most 3 visible, the newest in front, each older one moved up 14px and scaled down 5% per step, with its text hidden. Expand the stack on hover with 14px gaps and pause the timers while it's open. Toasts last about 4 seconds, are 356px wide, and use an `aria-live="polite"` region. Never place them over the main action. ## Quick checks before you ship - Does anything move or jump after the page first loads? - Does every drag stay 1 to 1 under the pointer, and does every drag have a tap or keyboard alternative? - Can people undo instead of confirming, and does every dialog name its action? - Does every wait longer than about 10 seconds show real progress? - Are form errors shown when people leave a field, never while they're typing? - Does every change people need to notice happen where they're looking? - Does it still work with `prefers-reduced-motion` turned on?
